## The problem

Online misogyny is rarely just a list of insults. Often it is carried by neutral words used in a pejorative context,
by sarcasm, or by a threat phrased without a single vulgar word. The question of the thesis was simple: how well, and
with which limits, can a text classifier recognise it in Italian social media language?

I used the dataset of the [AMI task at EVALITA 2020](https://amievalita2020.github.io/): 13,115 tweets annotated by hand.
I kept only the binary label (misogynistic or not): 58.4% of the tweets are non-misogynistic and 41.6% misogynistic, a
mild imbalance that makes macro-F1 a better yardstick than accuracy.

## Cleaning a very noisy language

Tweets are full of slang, typos, emoji and abbreviations, so most of the work went into the preprocessing pipeline:

- **Cleaning:** URLs and mentions replaced by placeholders, emoji collapsed into a single token, punctuation removed
  except `!` and `?`, which carry emphasis.
- **Normalisation:** lowercasing, a dictionary that expands common abbreviations (*xkè* → *perché*), and elongated words
  shortened (*ciaooo* → *ciao*).
- **Lemmatisation** with spaCy, so that all the forms of a verb or noun share one token.
- **Disambiguation:** the step I found most interesting. Words like *cagna* mean "female dog" in one context and are a
  slur in another. For a lexicon of such words I defined two textual "anchors", one neutral and one pejorative, and used
  a zero-shot NLI model (mDeBERTa) to decide which anchor fits the tweet. The ambiguous word is then replaced by that
  anchor, so the downstream models see the meaning, not just the word.

The pipeline cut the vocabulary from 20,007 to 13,342 unique words (−33%) and the share of out-of-vocabulary tokens,
measured against the AlBERTo vocabulary, from 18.3% to 10.8%.

## Two families of models

**Classical models on TF-IDF features.** Multinomial Naive Bayes, logistic regression, linear SVM, decision tree and
random forest, each with a feature-selection step and tuned by grid search with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set.

**A CNN on word embeddings.** Pretrained GloVe vectors learned on tweets (100 dimensions), kept trainable so they can
adapt to the jargon of the dataset, followed by a 1D convolution, global max pooling, dropout and a sigmoid output.
I deliberately kept it small to limit overfitting, and trained it with early stopping on a validation set.

The data was split once, stratified, into 60% training, 20% validation and 20% test; the test set was used only for the
final evaluation.

## Same score, different mistakes

The random forest's most important features are explicit insults and words that objectify the female body. That makes it
an excellent detector of open abuse, and a fragile one everywhere else:

- *"Beh, non mi sento sottomessa o malmenata"* ("Well, I don't feel subjugated or beaten up") is flagged as misogynistic:
  the model reacts to *malmenata* and ignores the *non* in front of it.
- *"Ti apro in due"* ("I'll split you in two") is missed: no single word is offensive, the violence is in the construction.

The CNN reads short word sequences through its convolutions, so it can partly learn that a negation flips the meaning of
the verb after it, and its fine-tuned embeddings pick up the dataset's slang. It is not immune to these errors, but it is
better placed to handle them.

## What I took away

- **A strong baseline is a real result.** With careful preprocessing and feature selection, a random forest was within
  0.001 macro-F1 of the neural model. Any claim that a model is "better" needs that baseline next to it.
- **Look at the errors, not only the score.** Two models with the same F1 failed on very different tweets, and that tells
  you much more about what they learned.
- **Context is the hard part.** Negation, sarcasm and implicit threats are exactly where both models struggled. The natural
  next step is contextual representations: using the hidden states of a pretrained language model as features, or
  fine-tuning one directly — the direction I then followed in my master's.
